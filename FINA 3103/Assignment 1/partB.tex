\subsubsection{The Risky Portfolio}\label{the-risky-portfolio}

The weights of Stock Fund (\(S\)) and Bond Fund (\(B\)) in the
Sharpe-maximising portfolio \(P^*\) are given by:

\[w_S^* = \frac{R_S \sigma_B^2 - R_B \sigma_{SB}}{R_S \sigma_B^2 + R_B \sigma_S^2 - (R_S + R_B) \sigma_{SB}}\]

where \(R_S = E[r_S] - r_f\) and \(R_B = E[r_B] - r_f\) are the excess
returns and \(\sigma_{SB} = \rho_{SB}\sigma_S\sigma_B\).

\[w_B^* = 1 - w_S^*\]

The weights are independent of the investor's risk preference. This is
because it is the mean-variance optimal mix of two risky assets when we
consider their expected return and their covariance matrix. The capital
allocation line has constant Sharpe ratio. Different points represent
different combinations of the risk-asset and the risky portfolio. Where
an investor sits on the line depends on their risk preference \#\#\#
Optimal Capital Allocation to the Risky Portfolio (\(y^*\))

The fraction of wealth allocated to the optimal risky portfolio, denoted
as \(y^*\), is derived by maximizing the investor's mean-variance
utility function \(U(y)\) with respect to \(y\):

\[U(y) = E[r_C] - \frac{1}{2} A \sigma_C^2\]

Substituting the expected return \(E[r_C] = r_f + y(E[r_{P^*}] - r_f)\)
and variance \(\sigma_C^2 = y^2 \sigma_{P^*}^2\) of the complete
portfolio into the utility function yields:

\[U(y) = r_f + y\left(E[r_{P^*}] - r_f\right) - \frac{1}{2} A y^2 \sigma_{P^*}^2\]

To locate the utility-maximizing allocation, we evaluate the
\textbf{first-order condition (FOC)} by setting the first derivative of
\(U(y)\) with respect to \(y\) to zero:

\[\frac{dU}{dy} = \left(E[r_{P^*}] - r_f\right) - A y \sigma_{P^*}^2 = 0\]

Solving for \(y\) gives the optimal allocation formula:

\[y^* = \frac{E[r_{P^*}] - r_f}{A \sigma_{P^*}^2}\]

    \subsection{Question 3}\label{question-3}

\subsubsection{Different Borrowing and Lending
Rates}\label{different-borrowing-and-lending-rates}

With a borrowing rate \(r_{\text{borrow}} = 6\%\) above the lending rate
\(r_f = 3\%\), the capital allocation line has a kink at \(y = 1\). The
expected return of the complete portfolio \(\text{C}\) becomes

\[E[r_C] = \begin{cases} r_f + y\left(E[r_{P^*}] - r_f\right), & y \le 1 \quad \text{(lending)} \\ r_{\text{borrow}} + y\left(E[r_{P^*}] - r_{\text{borrow}}\right), & y > 1 \quad \text{(borrowing)} \end{cases}\]

The borrowing cost only applies to the borrowed amount \((y-1)\), and
\(\sigma_C = y\,\sigma_{P^*}\) is unchanged. Beyond the kink the CAL is
flatter, with slope
\(\left(E[r_{P^*}] - r_{\text{borrow}}\right)/\sigma_{P^*}\).

Applying the FOC on each segment gives three cases:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \(y_L = \dfrac{E[r_{P^*}] - r_f}{A\sigma_{P^*}^2} \le 1\): the
  investor lends and is unaffected.
\item
  \(y_B = \dfrac{E[r_{P^*}] - r_{\text{borrow}}}{A\sigma_{P^*}^2} \ge 1\):
  the investor borrows at \(r_{\text{borrow}}\), and \(y^* = y_B\).
\item
  \(y_B < 1 < y_L\): the investor wants to borrow at \(r_f\) but not at
  \(r_{\text{borrow}}\), so \(y^* = 1\).
\end{enumerate}

Case 3 gives the range of \(A\) for which the investor holds exactly
100\% in \(P^*\):

\[\frac{E[r_{P^*}] - r_{\text{borrow}}}{\sigma_{P^*}^2} \le A \le \frac{E[r_{P^*}] - r_f}{\sigma_{P^*}^2}\]

Utility is evaluated with the rate of the segment the investor ends up
on: \(U = E[r_C] - \tfrac{1}{2}A y^2 \sigma_{P^*}^2\).

    \subsection{Question 4}\label{question-4}

\textbf{Is the advice consistent with (b)?} No.~In (b) every member
holds stocks and bonds in the same ratio,
\(w_S/w_B = 0.4624/0.5376 = 0.86\), and risk aversion only changes
\(y^*\), the split between \(P^*\) and the risk-free asset. This is the
separation property: under mean-variance utility with a single rate for
borrowing and lending, a more risk-averse investor should hold more
cash, not more bonds. The advisers' rule contradicts this.

\textbf{Reason 1: Borrowing costs more than lending (supported by our
numbers in (c)).} Separation needs investors to borrow and lend at the
same rate. With \(r_{\text{borrow}} = 6\%\), leverage on \(P^*\) only
earns \(7.85\% - 6\% = 1.85\%\) per unit, and the slope of the CAL
beyond \(P^*\) drops from 0.44 to 0.17. An investor who wants more risk
than \(P^*\) can instead get it without borrowing, by tilting the risky
portfolio towards stocks. For our group:

Both are better off leaving \(P^*\), and the optimal mix depends on
\(A\): the less risk-averse Chen holds almost only stocks, while Elaine
holds more bonds. Investors with \(A \geq 4.02\), who lend, keep
\(P^*\). In this setting the advisers' rule is rational.

\textbf{Reason 2: Preferences depend on more than mean and variance
(supported by our numbers in (a)).} Bets Y and Z have the same mean
(7\%) and the same standard deviation (16\%). Under mean-variance
utility, each member should therefore give them the same CE. None of us

The bets differ only in skewness. Y has a small chance of a large loss
(negative skew), and Z has a small chance of a large gain (positive
skew). Our answers show that we care about this third moment, and our
implied \(A\) is not even constant across bets. \(P^*\) is optimal only
for an investor who looks at mean and variance alone. An investor who
dislikes left-tail risk, as Elaine does by valuing Z above Y, would
rationally hold fewer of the assets with crash risk (typically stocks)
and more bonds than in \(P^*\).

\emph{Other reasons (not required):} hedging of future liabilities or a
long horizon, for which long-term bonds are close to risk-free;
correlation with labour income; and estimation error in \(E[r]\), which
makes the extreme weights from mean-variance optimisation unreliable.
